Picture a young woman in Lucknow who wants to open a small bakery. She registers online in the morning, follows a published food-safety spec, and sells her first loaf the same week. Nothing about her story is grand, and yet ten ideas sit quietly behind it. I want to set them out once, clearly, because they explain why each chapter makes the choices it does. They are directions that guide action, open to refinement, and each one is already proven somewhere, in India or abroad.

## One country, one law

A citizen in Kerala and a citizen in Kashmir deserve the same rights and the same rules. So do a small trader and a large company. When one law applies to everyone, every person knows where they stand, and every business can plan for the whole country at once.

One law also keeps the rules short. Each special carve-out by caste, religion, region, occupation or size adds a new layer of paperwork, a new office to manage it, and a new reason to lobby. A single, equal rule is fairer, simpler and easier to enforce. A published threshold that applies alike to everyone above it, such as a size above which a building needs a fire engineer, fits this principle well, because it treats every case the same way.

## Minimum government, maximum governance

I find the state does its best work when it focuses. Defence, justice, intelligence, policing and the core public networks are jobs only a state can do, and India deserves to have them done superbly.

Everything else thrives in open, competitive markets, where startups and established firms alike can serve citizens and compete to do it better. The state still buys at published prices for its own core needs, such as defence, space, nuclear power and the grain buffer. It pays for rights and public goods, like schooling, legal aid and identity, so every citizen has them. A smaller, sharper state can pay its people well, hold them to results and earn the trust of the citizens it serves.

The best civil post is the one that is never needed. Every task is automated first, contracted out second, and kept in-house only when neither works, so the number of posts and the wage bill fall every year and the savings return to citizens as lower taxes.

The same goes for laws. Every law carries a 10-year sunset: it lapses on its own unless Parliament renews it after a public review. A law has to keep earning its place, so the rulebook stays as lean as the state. The same rule runs through the whole state: everything expires unless it re-earns its place. Laws lapse after 10 years and every continuous government role after 5, unless renewed with a published reason (Chapters 8 and 12).

And government works as a platform, not a provider. Just as NPCI built UPI and let PhonePe, Google Pay and others compete to serve every user, each department builds open, interoperable platforms with published standards and prices. Any certified company or startup picks up a job, delivers it and is paid per verified result, citizens rate every delivery, and every record is searchable. The policymaker sets the policy and the guardrails; the builders deliver ([Chapter 12](/the-third-freedom/a-lean-state-that-delivers)).

And every law looks forward. A law, a tax or a rule applies from the day it is made, never to deals already done, so a business can plan for years with certainty. The one narrow exception is a deliberate scheme that defeats a law's plain purpose: an independent tribunal can undo the gain from it, on clear proof, without penalty for the past and never to raise revenue.

## Register, don't ask permission

The licence raj ended in 1991 for large industry. The third freedom ends it for everyone else. Indian businesses today juggle tens of thousands of compliance requirements, and each one is a cost, a delay and a chance for a bribe.

So the new default is simple: register online with the business's own PAN, which becomes its Business ID with no new ID needed (a sole proprietor gets a free business PAN in minutes), and start the same day. Rules are published as clear specs for safety, fire, food and buildings. The owner certifies that they follow them and carries full responsibility for any harm, backed by insurance and random audits. A short list of truly dangerous activities, such as nuclear materials, explosives, medicines and banking, keeps a licence, decided within a fixed time with written reasons. This trusts honest people, who are the great majority, and frees the state to focus on real risks.

## The state buys at a fair published price, speed first

Most of what the state buys has a clear, known cost: roads, pipes, power lines, devices, buildings. For all of these, the state publishes the spec and a fair cost-plus price, set by independent cost experts as the audited cost plus a fixed margin. Any certified firm that meets the spec and posts a bond can take the work. Payment follows independent inspection, and cheating carries a heavy, certain price.

This replaces lowest-bid tenders, which add months, invite rigging and reward cutting corners. With the price fixed and public, firms compete on quality, speed and coverage. I have come to believe time is the most valuable resource a country has: a road finished a year early is worth far more in growth than the last rupee saved on its price.

Auctions keep one important role. When the state sells a scarce public asset, such as land, mineral blocks or spectrum, an open auction to the highest bidder secures full value for the public.

## The Aadhaar way of rolling out

Aadhaar gave more than a billion people a digital identity in under a decade, at a cost of about ₹78 per ID (estimate). It did this with a simple structure: a small central authority that sets the rules, many registrars and certified agencies that do the work, open specs that any certified device can meet, and payment for each verified result.

We already know this works at Indian scale, so every national rollout in this book follows the same pattern, from scanning land records to installing chargers to setting up food labs. Competition among many players drives prices down and quality up. Public dashboards show results by district and agency. The book also builds in the lessons Aadhaar taught: rates that cover real costs, payment within 30 days, free service for every citizen, and a bonus for reaching the hardest districts first.

India's best companies show the other half: how to build big, fast and on budget. Reliance built the Jamnagar refinery, then the largest single industrial investment by an Indian group, in a record **33 months**. Jio signed up **100 million** customers in **170 days**, about 7 every second. Tata Motors built its Sanand car plant in **14 months**. The habits behind these are simple, and every mission in this book borrows them: one owner answerable for each project from start to finish, the budget and the finish date fixed before work begins, long-lead equipment ordered on day one, standard designs repeated rather than redrawn, many teams working in parallel, and a daily dashboard of cost and progress against plan that the leader reads every morning.

## Sovereignty first

A great nation keeps the keys to its own house. India's security and mission-critical systems, such as defence production, intelligence, the payment rails, the power grid's control centres, air traffic control and the digital public infrastructure, are owned, run and keyed by Indians, with their data stored in India.

This makes India a confident partner. Foreign firms remain welcome as suppliers, licensors and minority investors, and allied engineers can work on these systems under Indian management. India gains the world's best technology while keeping full control of the systems its people depend on. And every Indian company and startup can enter these sectors and grow.

## Pay for outcomes

What the state pays for is what it gets. When it pays for activity, it gets activity: tonnes of garbage moved, forms filled, meetings held. When it pays for outcomes, it gets results.

So this book pays for results that matter to citizens. Clean-air work is paid on measured air quality. A rollout is paid per verified enrolment. A school is measured on what children learn. Paying for outcomes rewards the firms and officials who deliver, and lets you see exactly what each rupee achieved.

## No cap on dreams

I would love to see India's companies grow as large as Google or Apple and lead the world. Every global giant began as a small firm that was allowed to keep growing. Scale brings research budgets, global reach and millions of jobs.

So this book sets no market-share or sector caps on any business. The state polices conduct instead: cartels, predatory pricing and the misuse of a dominant position are met firmly by a strong competition regulator. Firms grow by serving customers better, and customers keep the full benefit of fair competition. Growth is welcome; fair play is required.

## Europe for quality of life, America for capitalism

Why reinvent what the world has already proven? For the things that touch our bodies and our daily lives, Europe sets the world's highest bar. For the freedom to build, fund, fail and grow, America has no equal. So this book takes the best of each as India's reference.

Europe is the benchmark for chemicals, pesticides, food quality and quality of life. Every pesticide and chemical that Europe does not allow phases out of India, with a safer alternative named for each use. Food meets European residue limits, and air and noise follow European limits on a published phase-in path. A family in Nashik eats food as safe as a family in Munich.

America is the benchmark for capitalism. Starting a company takes a day, closing one is quick, and failure is a fresh start rather than a stain. Deep capital markets, venture funding, stock options for employees and listing on an Indian exchange as easily as on Nasdaq let a founder in Indore raise money as freely as one in California, and grow without limit.

Each chapter names the European or American standard it adopts, phases it in within 1 year where it can, and publishes the gap that remains. India sets its own laws, and these two benchmarks tell everyone, at home and abroad, exactly where the bar sits.

## Every rule has a guardrail

Bold reforms earn trust when citizens can see them working. So every rule in this book comes with a guardrail that can be measured: a number, a deadline or a clear consequence. Data is published live. Decisions carry written reasons. Officials and firms answer for results.

Guardrails are what make speed safe. They let the state move fast while giving every citizen the means to check that power is used well. They turn good intentions into commitments that anyone can verify.

## How the principles work together

These ten ideas reinforce one another. One law and registration in place of permission make starting simple. Fair published prices and the Aadhaar way make building fast. Paying for outcomes and clear guardrails make every rupee count. Sovereignty first keeps India in control, and no cap on dreams lets Indian firms lead the world. A lean, focused state ties them together.

Go back to the baker in Lucknow. One law, one registration, one published spec, and a state that checks results rather than paperwork: that is all she needed. Each chapter that follows applies these principles to one part of national life. Read together, they describe a country where the state does a few things superbly and its people are free to do everything else.
