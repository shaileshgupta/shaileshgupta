## The plan

1.  **One fixed PM2.5 path for every city and district.**
    -   Baseline: each city's and district's 2025 annual PM2.5, weather-adjusted, published in the first 30 days.
    -   Path: at least 10% lower every year, weather-adjusted. The WHO interim targets (35, 25, 15 and 10 µg/m³) and the WHO guideline (5 µg/m³) are the published milestones; the national standard (40 µg/m³ annual, 60 µg/m³ for 24 hours) is the floor every city passes first.
    -   **The European destination.** The revised EU Ambient Air Quality Directive (EU) 2024/2881, in force since 10 December 2024, sets annual limits to be met by 2030 of 10 µg/m³ for PM2.5 (down from 25), 20 µg/m³ for PM10 (down from 40) and 20 µg/m³ for nitrogen dioxide (down from 40). Within 1 year, India's National Ambient Air Quality Standards are amended to write these values in as the legal destination, applying from the date of the amendment onward, with today's national values as the first step and the 10% yearly path as the route between them. Each city's gap to the EU values is published with its baseline and updated every January.
    -   When the EU revises its limits at its reviews (by 31 December 2030 and every 5 years after), India's destination follows at the next national review, again applying forward only.
    -   At 10% a year, the national average of about 41 µg/m³ reaches about 25 by year 5, 15 by year 10, the EU 2030 limit of 10 by about year 14 and 5 by year 20 (estimate). Beijing fell from 89.5 to 30.5 µg/m³ from 2013 to 2024, about 9% a year (estimate).
    -   Air moves across borders, so each airshed (for example the Indo-Gangetic plain) gets one joint plan, and each state's share of the path is published.
2.  **A live sensor in every block and every city ward.**
    -   Every block and every urban ward gets at least one certified low-cost PM2.5 and PM10 sensor, reporting every 15 minutes.
    -   Every city of 1 lakh people or more (about 600, [Chapter 20](/the-third-freedom/built-right-built-to-last)) gets at least one reference-grade station, which also serves as the calibration point for nearby sensors.
    -   All data flows to one open national dashboard run by the Central Pollution Control Board, with a free open interface, raw data and calibration logs downloadable by anyone.
    -   Sensors and stations are bought at a published price from any certified vendor who posts a bond, and paid monthly only for verified uptime of at least 95% (How the government buys).
3.  **Open citizen monitoring around any polluter.**
    -   **Open specification, no licence.** CPCB and CSIR-NPL publish an open sensor specification: accuracy band against a reference station, data format, signed readings, GPS location and tamper log. Any maker can build to it.
    -   **Certification by test.** A sensor model is certified after a co-location test against a reference station at any accredited lab, such as the CSTEP Indi-SET facility in Bengaluru. Each installed unit self-registers on the dashboard with its location, owner and calibration date, and is re-calibrated every 6 months by co-location or a travelling reference unit.
    -   **Who can install.** Any NGO, resident group, school, university, company CSR team or public-private body can place certified sensors around any factory, kiln, power plant, dump, construction site or road, on public land with notice to the city, or on private land with the owner's consent. Funding comes from the group itself or from CSR, since Schedule VII item (iv) of the Companies Act already covers environmental sustainability and the quality of air.
    -   **Trigger.** When three or more certified sensors near one source read above 90 µg/m³ of PM2.5 (1.5 times the 24-hour standard) for 3 days running, while upwind sensors read lower, the dashboard raises an automatic alert.
    -   **Reference check.** Within 7 days, an accredited lab assigned at random through the faceless audit system ([Chapter 13](/the-third-freedom/clean-money-trusted-public-life)) measures at the site with reference-grade equipment and, for a stack source, checks the plant's own stack sensor.
    -   **Action.** A confirmed breach brings the hourly fee schedule (item 4) from the date of confirmation and the closure steps for repeat breaches (item 4). The polluter pays the cost of the check, and 10% of the confirmed fee goes to the sensor's owner to keep the network running.
    -   **Safeguards.** Signed readings and location logs; a sensor that moves or loses its seal stops counting. Tampering or planted readings mean loss of certification for the group and its directors for life, and prosecution. A group with 3 unconfirmed alerts in a year pays for its next reference check. The site owner sees every alert at the same moment as the public and can submit its own reference data.
4.  **Industry pays for its smoke.**
    -   **Live sensors.** Every red-category industry (the most polluting class), thermal plant, cement plant, brick-kiln cluster and large construction site has a certified sensor that reports every 15 minutes, with all data public on the live map. Delhi is still fitting online monitors at 2,354 industries; the national sensor order gives every red-category unit 90 days to connect.
    -   **Automatic hourly fees.** Each hour over the limit costs a fixed fee: ₹10,000 for small units, ₹1 lakh for large units and ₹5 lakh for power plants, debited by e-challan. The Jan Vishwas Act, 2023 already turned these environmental offences into penalties set by an adjudicating officer. Parliament amends the penalty sections of the Air Act, the Water Act and the Environment Protection Act in its first session to add this hourly schedule. Until the amendment is in force, officers levy the maximum penalties the Acts already allow.
    -   **Three breaches and the plant is sealed.** Three confirmed breach-days in 90 days lead to a 7-day notice, then sealing under the closure powers of the Air Act and the Environment Protection Act. The plant reopens only after an independent lab confirms the fix. Limits are each unit's legally notified norms on day 1, so a coal plant with a notified scrubber date pays on sulphur dioxide from that date (item 5), and on dust and nitrogen oxides at once.
    -   **One rule for essential supply.** Where the grid operator or water utility certifies that sealing a unit would cut grid power, water or hospital supply, the fee doubles every 90 days instead, for at most 12 months. The grid operator publishes a replacement plan within 30 days, and the unit is sealed once replacement supply is certified. Fees are never passed on to consumers through power tariffs.
    -   Emissions trading in the 10 largest industrial clusters within 1 year, on the Surat model: a cluster cap that falls every year, permits that plants buy and sell, and live stack monitors as the measure. Any permits sold by the state are a permit cost, and the money goes to the City Clean Air Fund.
    -   Brick kilns move to cleaner designs on a published date, with cluster sensors. A shared cluster sensor's fee is split by capacity, unless a unit proves compliance with its own sensor.
5.  **Every coal plant fits sulphur scrubbers.**
    -   The July 2025 relaxation, which exempted about 78% of plants, is replaced by a single rule: every coal unit has a published flue-gas desulphurisation (FGD) date. Category A units keep their 2027 deadline; every other unit's date is published in the first 30 days, staged by unit age and distance from cities.
    -   From that date, every hour of sulphur dioxide above the notified norm carries the hourly fee. Units due to close under [Chapter 19](/the-third-freedom/energy-power-made-in-india)'s nuclear and coal-retirement plan may choose an earlier closure date instead of a retrofit.
    -   The plant owner pays for the scrubber and recovers it through the regulated tariff as a change in law.
6.  **Straw becomes fuel.**
    -   Certified custom-hiring operators rent residue machines by the hour at a published price; buyers pay the farmer at the field gate.
    -   Every coal plant co-fires 5% to 10% biomass under the existing Ministry of Power policy, with paddy straw pellets first for plants in the north; 71 plants already co-fire. Pellet makers, bale aggregators and biogas plants buy straw at a published floor offered by the buying plants.
    -   A farm fire confirmed by satellite carries an environmental charge on the land record, as CAQM already allows.
7.  **Cleaner vehicles and roads.**
    -   Vehicles make up about 51% to 53% of Delhi's local PM2.5 emissions in mid-November (CSE analysis of IITM data), so the city fleet matters most. Electric fleets and highway chargers as in [Chapter 19](/the-third-freedom/energy-power-made-in-india).
    -   Roadside remote-sensing cameras test tailpipes on main roads. A vehicle that fails twice gets 30 days to repair or exit to a registered scrapping facility.
    -   The ten most polluted cities run low-emission zones with a daily charge on older vehicles, as London does.
    -   India jumped from BS-IV straight to BS-VI fuel and vehicles on 1 April 2020. The next standard after BS-VI is notified with a fixed date within 1 year.
8.  **Dust, homes and waste.**
    -   Every construction site above 500 square metres carries a certified dust sensor and covers on all loose material; a site over its limit pays the hourly fee.
    -   Roads are swept by machine. Any contractor certified to the open clean-up spec can work in any city at the published price (How the government buys), is paid on the measured PM10 drop at ward sensors, and is de-certified within 7 days if it fails an audit.
    -   Household solid fuel adds about 30% of India's outdoor PM2.5 (median of seven studies). Ujjwala has given about 10.33 crore connections, with about 4.47 refills a year on average in FY25. Every home cooks with gas or electricity. The per-cylinder Ujjwala support moves into the one income-tested cash transfer ([Chapter 23](/the-third-freedom/a-hand-up-and-an-equal-chance)), so families buy the fuel they choose.
    -   Ward waste operators are paid on zero open burning, confirmed by ward sensors and photo reports.
9.  **Clean-air money paid on results.** NCAP and Finance Commission air grants are paid to cities on the measured, weather-adjusted drop in PM2.5 against the path.

## Cost and return

| Item (estimate) | Arithmetic | Cost (₹ crore) | Who pays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Block and ward sensors | planning figure of 1 lakh sensors × ₹50,000 (the certified low-cost sensor price) | 500 | City Clean Air Funds and existing NCAP grants |
| Industrial stack sensors | about 20,000 stacks × ₹15 lakh | 3,000 | Industry |
| Small-source sensors | about 1.4 lakh brick kilns and small sites × about ₹50,000 | about 700 | Industry |
| Citizen sensors | at the owner's choice | as funded | NGOs, groups and CSR |

| Straw collection and pellets | market price | as traded | Buying plants and pellet makers |

-   **Polluters pay for the clean-up:** all fee revenue goes into a ring-fenced City Clean Air Fund. Industry pays for its stack and small-source sensors, about ₹3,700 crore in all (estimate).
-   **Technology:** online emission monitoring on the Central Pollution Control Board cloud, AI checks to catch tampering, and e-challans on the [Chapter 4](/the-third-freedom/digital-rails-for-every-citizen) rails, built by states and by any software firm that passes the open standard's certification test.
-   **Funds already flowing:** ₹13,415 crore has been released to cities under NCAP and Finance Commission air grants since 2019, 74% of it used and 68% of that on road dust. The same money moves to measured results.
-   **CSR headroom:** companies spent about ₹34,909 crore on CSR in FY24, with about 8.4% on the environment, about ₹2,900 crore (estimate). A small share funds a national citizen network.
-   **Health return:** air pollution cost about ₹2.71 lakh crore in 2019, about 1.36% of GDP. Each 10% of that burden removed is worth about ₹27,000 crore a year (estimate).
-   **Scrubbers:** a 2018 CSTEP study put their benefits at about ₹9.62 lakh crore, about 3.8 times the cost, over 90% from lives saved. A Harvard and IIT Hyderabad study estimates full FGD could cut PM2.5 by about 8% and avoid about 48,000 early deaths.
-   **Markets:** in Surat, plants in the market cut particulates by 20% to 30% at 11% lower abatement cost, with benefits at least 25 times the costs.
-   **Straw:** Punjab and Haryana produce about 26.6 million tonnes of paddy straw a season (18.80 plus 7.79, estimate), and already use about 10.6 million tonnes outside the field (estimate). India has about 230 million tonnes of surplus crop residue a year.

## Timeline

**First 30 days:**

-   Publish every city's and district's PM2.5 baseline and 10% yearly path, with its gap to the EU 2030 limits for PM2.5, PM10 and nitrogen dioxide.
-   Publish the open sensor specification and certification test; the national dashboard accepts data from any certified sensor.
-   Table the hourly-fee amendments to the Air, Water and Environment Protection Acts, and issue the sensor order: every red-category unit connects within 90 days.
-   Issue the order giving every coal unit a published FGD date and the hourly sulphur fee.
-   Move NCAP and Finance Commission air grants to payment on measured results.
-   Notify the citizen-alert trigger and the 7-day reference check rule.

**Within 1 year:**

-   A certified sensor reports live from every block and ward, and a reference station runs in every city of 1 lakh or more.
-   Emissions trading live in the 10 largest industrial clusters.
-   Straw buyers and co-firing contracts cover every paddy district of Punjab and Haryana.
-   Low-emission zones live in the ten most polluted cities, and remote tailpipe testing on their main roads.
-   100% of red-category units online, with hourly fees automatic.
-   Winter PM2.5 in the National Capital Region 15% lower than in 2025, weather-adjusted.
-   National Ambient Air Quality Standards amended with the EU 2030 limits as the legal destination.

**Outcomes after year 1:** every city on its 10% yearly path; national average PM2.5 about 25 µg/m³ by year 5, 15 by year 10, the EU 2030 limit of 10 by about year 14 and close to 5 by year 20 (estimate); every coal unit fitted with scrubbers or retired.

## Guardrails

-   **The path:** a city that misses its yearly step two years running hands its clean-air plan to an independent certified operator paid on results; each January the results for every city are published.
-   **Open data:** every government and certified sensor reports at least every 15 minutes; a government sensor offline for more than 24 hours is repaired within 72 hours at the operator's cost, and an outage over 4 hours at a plant's stack sensor counts as a breach.
-   **Sensor quality:** stack sensors are certified by the Central Pollution Control Board and calibrated every 6 months by an accredited lab. Tampering with a stack sensor means sealing at once plus prosecution.
-   **Citizen alerts:** reference check within 7 days of an alert; a check missed by the deadline is reported publicly and the lab loses its accreditation for the next draw.
-   **False alarms and tampering:** loss of certification for life for tampering, and the cost of the next check after 3 unconfirmed alerts in a year.
-   **Appeals:** the adjudicating officer confirms a breach within 7 days. An appeal is filed within 15 days and decided within 30. Only 25% of a disputed fee is paid first, the rest only if the appeal fails, and refunds carry 9% a year interest.
-   **Workers:** while a plant is sealed, the owner keeps paying full wages.
-   **Scrubbers:** every hour of sulphur above the norm after the FGD date pays the hourly fee; the regulator publishes each unit's retrofit status every month.
-   **Polluter pays:** every fee, penalty and permit cost goes into a ring-fenced City Clean Air Fund; none of it is a tax.
-   **The European benchmark:** every January, each city's annual PM2.5, PM10 and nitrogen dioxide are published beside the EU 2030 limits, with the gap in µg/m³ and the year the city reaches each limit on its current path.

## Legal route

-   **National Ambient Air Quality Standards, 2009:** amended within 1 year to add the EU 2030 limits of Directive (EU) 2024/2881 as the legal destination, applied forward only.
-   **Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981:** state boards' powers to set emission standards, sample air and emissions, and issue directions including closure; penalties as amended by the Jan Vishwas Act, 2023. Parliament adds the hourly fee schedule to the Air Act, the Water Act and the Environment Protection Act in its first session.
-   **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986:** central power to set standards and issue directions; the FGD order and the open sensor specification are notified under it. Thermal plant sulphur norms sit in the Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986.
-   **Commission for Air Quality Management in NCR and Adjoining Areas Act, 2021:** directions for the National Capital Region, Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh on stubble, industry, co-firing and dust.
-   **Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 and Central Motor Vehicles Rules, 1989:** emission standards, remote-sensing tests and exit to registered scrapping facilities.
-   **Companies Act, 2013, section 135 and Schedule VII:** CSR funding of citizen sensor networks under item (iv).
-   **Electricity Act, 2003:** scrubber costs recovered through regulated tariffs; the Ministry of Power co-firing policy.

## Limits to know

Much of North India's winter smog is regional and weather-driven, so cities on the Indo-Gangetic plain depend on each other, and year-to-year results move with the weather; the path is judged weather-adjusted and over several years. For the same reason India cannot reach the WHO guideline within 1 year: the year-1 target for the National Capital Region is a 15% weather-adjusted cut in winter PM2.5, and deeper cuts come after year 1. The 10% yearly path is ambitious, about Beijing's pace, and cities starting far above the national average take longer. Europe itself allows a member state to postpone a 2030 limit, at the latest to 1 January 2040, with a published roadmap, so the EU limit is India's destination on a published path rather than a 2030 deadline. Low-cost sensors drift and read high in humid air (raw PurpleAir data read about 40% high in the United States before correction), so they trigger checks and never decide fines on their own; India's current instrument certification scheme has so far left sensor-based monitors out, so the open specification is new work. The count of blocks and wards used for the sensor budget is a planning figure to be confirmed. Scrubber retrofits take time and supply, so dates are staged. Biomass co-firing has faced supply and quality shortfalls, so straw buying depends on reliable pellet supply. Household fuel choice also depends on income, which the [Chapter 23](/the-third-freedom/a-hand-up-and-an-equal-chance) transfer supports.
